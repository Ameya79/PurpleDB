The Problem: Why Does PurpleDB Exist?

The nightmare of searching video by meaning with traditional tools

Imagine you have 1,000 hours of family videos, drone footage, or security recordings. You want to type: "a golden retriever puppy catching a frisbee on the beach" and immediately jump to the exact 5 seconds where that happened.

Why Text Databases (like SQL) Fail at Video Search

A relational database like MySQL or SQLite can only search exact text letters. If a file is named VID_20261114_0042.mp4, SQL knows nothing about what is inside. It cannot "see" the puppy, the sand, or the frisbee. Traditional search engines look for words; but video is an avalanche of millions of colored pixels changing 30 times every second.

How People Tried to Solve This (And Why It Sucks)

Before PurpleDB, if a company or developer wanted to search videos, they had to stitch together five completely separate, fragile software tools:

What "Embedded Database" Truly Means

Just like SQLite replaced giant database servers for phone apps and local software, PurpleDB is an embedded database. There is NO separate server process running in the background. There are NO network ports to configure, NO usernames or passwords, and NO internet calls required. It is a tiny, super-fast C++ engine compiled directly into your application. When your program starts, PurpleDB starts. When your program closes, PurpleDB closes.

Hardware Physical Reality: How Computers Actually Work

Bits, Bytes, RAM Mailboxes, and the Astronomical Speed Gap

To understand why PurpleDB is designed the way it is, we have to look inside the physical computer case. No hand-waving—let's understand the silicon.

1. What is a Bit and a Byte?

Inside a computer chip are billions of microscopic electronic switches called transistors. A transistor can either have electric voltage (ON = 1) or no voltage (OFF = 0). That single 0 or 1 is called a bit.

Because one bit can only say Yes or No, computers group 8 bits together into a Byte. One byte (like 01000001) can represent numbers from 0 to 255, or a single letter (like the letter 'A').

2. RAM: The Infinite Street of Numbered Mailboxes

Your computer has 16 Gigabytes or 32 Gigabytes of RAM (Random Access Memory). What is RAM physically? Imagine a giant, perfectly straight street with 16 billion houses. Every house has a mailbox, and every mailbox can hold exactly 1 Byte of data.

Figure 2: RAM is an indexed sequence of byte locations. 0x1000 is the hexadecimal address (street number).

The number on the mailbox (like 0x1000) is called a Memory Address. When your computer wants to read data, the CPU sends that exact street number along copper wires to fetch the byte inside.

3. The Astronomical Speed Gap: RAM vs. SSD vs. Hard Drive

Why don't we just save everything directly to the hard drive or SSD? Because of a brutal physical reality: The storage speed gap. If we scale 1 tick of your computer processor (1 nanosecond) to 1 human heart beat (1 second), look at how long the rest of the computer takes:

Hardware Component Actual Physical Time Scaled to Human Time What It Feels Like
CPU Register / L1 Cache 0.5 nanoseconds 0.5 seconds Blinking your eye
Main Memory (RAM) 50 nanoseconds 1 minute Walking into the kitchen for water
Fast NVMe SSD (Flash) 50,000 nanoseconds 14 hours Flying from New York to Tokyo
Old Mechanical Hard Drive 10,000,000 nanoseconds 4 months A semester at university
The Golden Rule of Database Engineering

Every single time a database has to touch the disk or SSD, it is like pausing for 14 hours instead of 1 minute. PurpleDB is fast because it is architected so the CPU almost NEVER has to wait on the SSD.

C++ From Scratch: No Jargon, Just Machines

Pointers, Memory Leaks, Garbage Collectors, and RAII Demystified

Many people fear C++ because tutorials use terrifying words like "polymorphism", "dangling pointers", and "rvalue references". Let's strip all the mysticism away. What is C++ actually doing?

1. What on Earth is a "Pointer"?

Remember the street of numbered mailboxes? Imagine you have a piece of paper in your pocket, and you write: "0x1000" on it. That piece of paper is a Pointer!

Pointer Analogy: The Treasure Map

A variable is the treasure chest itself (holding coins or data). A pointer is NOT the treasure chest—it is a tiny piece of paper with the GPS coordinates written on it. In C++, passing a pointer around takes 8 bytes of space. If you have a 100 Megabyte video frame, you don't copy all 100 Megabytes; you just hand someone a piece of paper with the address where it sits. This is called Zero-Copy.

2. What is "Memory Allocation"? The Stack vs. The Heap

When your code runs, where do variables live?

The Stack (The Fast Buffet Plates)

Imagine a stack of plates at a restaurant buffet. When a function starts, it puts a plate on top. When the function ends, that plate is popped off.

  • Speed: Lightning fast (a single CPU instruction).
  • Catch: Variables must have a fixed, known size ahead of time. You can't put a dynamic 1GB video on the stack!

The Heap (The Giant Rental Warehouse)

A massive warehouse of RAM. You ask the operating system: "Can you rent me 50 Megabytes of empty space right now?" The OS finds a spot and hands you the pointer address.

  • Advantage: Holds any dynamic size (video files, vector graphs).
  • Catch: YOU must tell the computer when you are done renting it!

3. What is a "Garbage Collector" (and why C++ avoids it)?

If you rent space in the warehouse (the Heap) and forget to give it back, that space is lost forever until your computer restarts. This is called a Memory Leak.

The Janitor Analogy (Garbage Collection vs. C++ RAII)

The Garbage Collector (Java / Python / Go): Imagine an office where employees drop empty soda cans on the floor all day. Every 10 minutes, a giant siren blares, the doors lock, and everyone MUST FREEZE ("Stop-the-World!"). A janitor walks through every cubicle, inspecting every single can to see if it's trash, and sweeps it into a bin. While the janitor works, all work halts! If you are streaming live video or running a fast search, the video stutters.

C++ RAII (Resource Acquisition Is Initialization): In C++, there is NO janitor. Every employee carries their soda can directly to the recycling bin the exact millisecond they finish drinking it. Memory is cleaned up instantly, deterministically, with ZERO pause sirens! That is why high-performance databases (SQLite, RocksDB, PurpleDB) are built in C++.

Multimodal Vectors: How Computers "Understand" Videos

Turning pixels and words into coordinates in a high-dimensional space

How can a computer know that a video frame of a cute puppy playing in sand has anything to do with the English words "beach dog"? The answer is Multimodal Vector Embeddings (using an AI vision-language model called CLIP).

1. What is a "Vector"?

Forget linear algebra textbooks. A vector is just an arrow pointing to a spot on a map! In 2 dimensions (like a city map), you have 2 numbers: $[X, Y]$ (e.g. 5 blocks East, 3 blocks North).

In PurpleDB, an AI model takes an image and converts it into a list of 512 numbers (a 512-dimensional vector). These 512 numbers represent conceptual coordinates in "Meaning Space".

What is "Cosine Similarity"?

To compare two concepts, we measure the angle ($\theta$) between their arrows:

  • Angle is $0^\circ$ ($\cos = 1.0$): Perfect match! Exactly the same concept.
  • Angle is $45^\circ$ ($\cos \approx 0.7$): Strongly related (e.g., "Golden retriever" and "Dog in grass").
  • Angle is $90^\circ$ ($\cos = 0.0$): Completely unrelated (e.g., "Dog" and "Formula 1 Car").

In PurpleDB, searching for a video means calculating this cosine similarity between your text query vector and thousands of video moment vectors using SIMD hardware acceleration ([`core/vector/distance.cc`](file:///c:/Users/vinay/OneDrive/Desktop/Purpled/core/vector/distance.cc)).

Critical Systems Insight: Subprocess Crash Isolation (ADR-031)

Why does PurpleDB run the external ffmpeg program inside an isolated child subprocess ([`core/pipeline/frame_decoder.cc`](file:///c:/Users/vinay/OneDrive/Desktop/Purpled/core/pipeline/frame_decoder.cc)) instead of directly linking an FFmpeg C library inside the database engine?

Why Untrusted Video Codecs Are Dangerous

Video compression formats (H.264, HEVC, AV1) are extraordinarily complex. Malicious or corrupt video files frequently contain exploit payloads designed to trigger buffer overflows or memory corruption inside media decoders. If PurpleDB decoded videos in its main memory, a single corrupt video file could crash the entire database or corrupt your data! By spawning an isolated child process, if FFmpeg crashes or runs out of memory, only the disposable child process dies. PurpleDB catches the exit code, logs an error, and the database remains 100% healthy.

The Storage Engine: Log-Structured Merge (LSM) Trees

Turning random SSD writes into ultra-fast sequential appends

Once video moments are clustered, how does PurpleDB store them on disk so they never get lost—even if the computer loses power mid-write? PurpleDB uses an LSM-Tree (Log-Structured Merge Tree), the same storage architecture behind Google's Bigtable and RocksDB.

What is a Bloom Filter? (The Bouncer Analogy)

Imagine a nightclub with 100 VIP guests inside. A million people walk up to the door and ask: "Is my friend Alice inside?" Checking the entire club takes 5 minutes. Instead, you hire a mathematical bouncer holding a tiny card with numbers. If the bouncer says: "NO, Alice is 100% definitely NOT inside", you leave immediately without wasting time searching!

Why Bloom Filters Matter in PurpleDB

In PurpleDB, an SSTable on disk can contain 50,000 keys. Before reading a single byte from the SSD, PurpleDB checks the Bloom Filter in memory ([`core/storage/filter_policy.cc`](file:///c:/Users/vinay/OneDrive/Desktop/Purpled/core/storage/filter_policy.cc)). For 99% of missing keys, the Bloom filter instantly rejects the lookup in 20 nanoseconds, preventing a 14-hour SSD read trip!

The Secret Sauce: WiscKey Separation & HNSW Graphs

Why traditional databases choke on vectors, and how PurpleDB searches in 6ms

1. The WiscKey Breakthrough: Key-Value Separation

In an LSM database, as data grows, background threads merge small files into larger files. This is called Compaction. If your database stores normal text keys (like a 20-byte username), compaction is fast.

BUT vector embeddings are huge! A 512-dimension vector takes 2,048 bytes. If you put vectors inside the LSM tree, compaction rewrites those giant 2KB vectors over and over again—up to 30 times! This destroys SSD flash memory and destroys write performance (high write amplification).

The WiscKey Architecture in PurpleDB

PurpleDB implements the landmark WiscKey pattern ([`core/vector/vector_segment.cc`](file:///c:/Users/vinay/OneDrive/Desktop/Purpled/core/vector/vector_segment.cc)):

  • Vectors are written once to an append-only file (.vec segment) and NEVER moved or rewritten during compaction.
  • The LSM Tree only holds a tiny pointer (file ID + byte offset, just 16 bytes).

Result: Write amplification drops from $30\times$ down to near $1\times$, and ingestion runs at maximum disk bandwidth!

2. HNSW: The Express Highway Graph for Vectors

If you have 100,000 video moments in your database, comparing your search query against every single vector takes too long. PurpleDB uses an HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) Proximity Graph ([`core/vector/hnsw.cc`](file:///c:/Users/vinay/OneDrive/Desktop/Purpled/core/vector/hnsw.cc)).
